# LLM-assisted code: the reasoning

This is the argument behind the two rules in
[CONTRIBUTING.md](CONTRIBUTING.md): that you submit code as yourself, and
that you do not mention a model in what you submit. You do not
need to read this to contribute. It is here because the rules are
consequences of a position, and a consequence is easier to apply — and
to argue with — when you can see what it came from.

## The framing

An LLM used for coding is a **nondeterministic compiler from an
undefined language into a target language**. That is not a thing anyone
ever actually wanted, but here we are with it.

Take the framing literally. The rules fall out of it.

## The input language is undefined

Not loosely specified — undefined. And you know what a conforming
compiler is permitted to do with undefined behaviour: anything at all.

If the reference isn't familiar: in February 1992 on the Usenet group
comp.std.c, John F. Woods was explaining why a particular construct had
no defined meaning, and wrote:

> In short, you can't use sizeof() on a structure whose elements haven't
> been defined, and if you do, demons may fly out of your nose.
>
> — John F. Woods, comp.std.c, "Re: Why is this legal?", 25 February 1992

He added immediately: "OK, OK; so the Standard doesn't \*ACTUALLY\*
mention demons or noses." The joke works because the Standard is barely
less permissive than that: permissible undefined behaviour *ranges from
ignoring the situation completely with unpredictable results* upward,
and there is no ceiling written anywhere. A follow-up poster coined
"nasal demons" and it stuck.

(The line usually quoted — "Permissible undefined behavior ranges from
ignoring the situation completely with unpredictable results, to having
demons fly out of your nose" — is a composite of the Standard's text and
Woods' punchline. It reads better than either. He didn't write it.)

There is no diagnostic, no guarantee, and no floor on how wrong the
output may be. With an undefined input language, *every* construct sits
in that territory.

## And the compiler is nondeterministic

This is the part that makes it genuinely worse than C.

Undefined behaviour in C is survivable in practice because the
implementation is fixed. You pin the compiler version, find out what
*your* toolchain actually does with a construct, test it, and rely on
that. Undefined in the Standard, defined in your build. That is how C
shops live with it.

None of that is available here. It will not read the same statement the
same way twice, nor produce a similar result twice. There is no version
to pin and no behaviour to learn, because there is no stable mapping to
learn *about*.

Two consequences worth naming:

**Trust cannot be amortised.** Normally you verify a tool once and then
rely on it; here you can only validate artifacts, one at a time, and the
cost never goes down. The output is the only thing you can reason about
— not the process that made it, and not the tool.

**Regenerating is not a fix.** A second attempt is a different program
with different bugs, not a corrected one. Rerolling until the tests pass
selects for output that passes your tests, which is not the same
property as output that is correct — and the gap between those two is
exactly where the demons live.

LLMs are nasal demon generation machines, now more than ever.

## Engineer accordingly

The rational response to a compiler like that is not to trust it, and
not to avoid it. It is to **do whatever is necessary to ensure the
product of your compiler matches your intent.** Read the output. Test
it. Understand why it is shaped the way it is. Check the cases it
didn't.

That is not diligence theatre or an ethics exercise. It is the only
thing standing between you and a nasal demon, and it is the same work
you would do for any tool whose output you cannot take on faith.

## Why the rules say what they say

From the above it follows that an LLM is not an independent agent and
should not be thought of as one. It does not author, it does not decide,
and it cannot be responsible — responsibility is not a property a tool
can hold.

So **you submit code as yourself.** You carry final responsibility for
anything you publish here, whether you typed every character or
generated all of it. "The model wrote it" explains nothing and excuses
less; it is precisely as much of a defence as "the compiler emitted it."

And **you do not mention a model in what you submit.** A tool is not
named in a commit, whether it is an editor, a compiler or a model.
`Co-Authored-By` trailers naming one, "generated by" lines and notes of
assistance assert a co-author who cannot answer for the work, and they
blur exactly the line the first rule exists to keep sharp. Your name
on the commit is the statement that you answer for the work, and it is
the only one.
